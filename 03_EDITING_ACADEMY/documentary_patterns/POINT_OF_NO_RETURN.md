# Pattern: POINT_OF_NO_RETURN

## Purpose
Name the moment the catastrophe became inevitable — on screen, as a chapter, as a graphic beat (Bible §4.4). The point of no return converts escalation into inevitability: the viewer stops hoping and starts watching the trap close.

## When to use
Every episode. Placed between the warnings/escalation and the catastrophe. If the record supports a precise moment (a valve closed, a course held, a threshold crossed), name it precisely. If the record is ambiguous, name the *window* honestly.

## When NOT to use
Never skip — but never invent. If no single point is defensible from the record, the beat becomes "the point of no return *window*" with the honest framing. Manufacturing a precise moment the evidence doesn't support is an honesty-gate failure.

## Narrative situation
End of the escalation block (template ~9:00–10:00). The last beat before the catastrophe.

## Visual sequence
1. The timeline graphic returns: the marker appears — a distinct line/color the viewer hasn't seen before.
2. Chapter title on screen: "Past the Point of No Return" (or the episode's equivalent).
3. The visual that embodies it: the last reading, the final position, the threshold diagram — one image, held.
4. Hold 2–3 seconds of near-stillness. Then the catastrophe begins.

## Typical duration
15–30 seconds. Short. The brevity is the point — inevitability doesn't need elaboration.

## Motion strategy
Stillness. The timeline marker appears; the image holds. Any motion decelerates into the hold. This is the calmest 20 seconds before the catastrophe.

## Transition strategy
Hard cut from the final warning into the point-of-no-return beat. Then a held beat — and the catastrophe enters without a transition flourish. The stillness *is* the transition.

## Sound strategy
[RESEARCH GAP.] Conservative: the drone drops out or thins to near-silence. The point of no return should feel quiet — the noise was the warnings; the silence is the inevitability.

## Common mistakes
- Skipping the beat (escalation runs straight into catastrophe — the viewer never feels the trap *close*).
- Over-explaining it (the point named is enough; a paragraph of narration deflates it).
- The point as a surprise twist (it should feel *recognized*, not sprung — the warnings built to it).
- Inventing precision ("at 11:57:03 the dam was doomed" when the record says "late morning").

## Chain-of-Failure application
Our template's hinge: everything before the point is *decisions*; everything after is *physics*. The edit must make the viewer feel that hinge. The timeline marker, the chapter title, the held image, the thinning sound — four signals, one moment, then the fall.

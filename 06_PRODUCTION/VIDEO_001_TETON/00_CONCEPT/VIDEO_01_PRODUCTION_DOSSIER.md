## 1. STORY RESEARCH (organized from the verified evidence pack)

Full evidence: `~/workspace/youtube-niche-research/video-01/teton-evidence-pack.md` (9 sections, 35-row claim ledger). Load-bearing verified facts for this dossier:

**The dam.** 305 ft high above the riverbed, 3,100-ft crest, zoned earthfill with central impervious core, Teton River canyon, Idaho. Authorized September 7, 1964 (PL 88-583). Built by Morrison-Knudsen, Feb 1972–Nov 1975. Purposes: flood control, power, recreation, irrigation. The Bureau of Reclamation's newest dam — an agency with 300+ major dams and, per the Department of the Interior, **no fatal dam failure before or since** (FACT).

**The filling.** First filling began October 3, 1975, at 1 ft/day. March 23, 1976: authorized increase to 2 ft/day (FACT — USBR project history; heavy snowpack year, limited outlet capacity — only the 850-cfs emergency outlet works was operational). **The "4 ft/day" figure in secondary sources is DISPUTED — never use it.** The Independent Panel explicitly found the filling rate **was NOT a material cause**: "a slower rate of filling would have delayed the failure but… a similar failure would have occurred at some later date" (FACT). The script must correct, not repeat, the popular filling-rate theory.

**The cause.** "The dam failed by **internal erosion (piping)** of the core of the dam deep in the **right foundation key trench**" (FACT — the panel's exact terminology). Two suspect initiation mechanisms — unsealed rock joints beneath the grout cap near Sta. 14+00, or hydraulic fracturing of the key-trench fill — were **never resolved**; the panel states it probably never will be (FACT). **The script must state this uncertainty plainly** — it is our honesty differentiator.

**June 5, 1976 (MDT, FACT unless noted).** Dam unattended overnight (DOCUMENTED). ~7:00–8:00 a.m.: muddy leakage discovered at the right abutment. 8:30 a.m.: formal notice — two leaks (2 cfs and 50 cfs); dozers directed. 9:30 a.m.: Robison considers alerting downstream residents, holds off — judged emergency not imminent, feared undue panic (DOCUMENTED; he was formally exonerated — frame as reasonable judgment under unknowable time pressure, never villainy). 10:00 a.m.: a 15-cfs tunnel emerges **inside the embankment** at Sta. 15+25 — erosion is now in the dam body. 10:00–10:45 a.m.: sheriffs notified, evacuation begins. 11:00 a.m.: whirlpool in the reservoir at Sta. 14+00. 11:30 a.m.: two dozers swallowed by the expanding sinkhole; operators pulled out by ropes. 11:55 a.m.: crest collapse. **11:57 a.m.: full breach** (FACT — Robison's report and US DHS independently).

**The flood.** A wall of water (contemporary press: ~15 ft — DOCUMENTED) struck Wilford and Sugar City ~1:00 p.m., Rexburg (~12 miles downstream) ~1:40 p.m. — ~80% of Rexburg inundated. 150–200 square miles flooded; contained 70 miles downstream at American Falls Reservoir. Warnings went out ~1 hour before the breach; **10 of the 11 dead had been warned** (DOCUMENTED — US DHS).

**Toll.** **11 deaths** — the official, convergent figure (USBR, USGS, US DHS) (FACT). The panel's own Dec 1976 report said 14 — preliminary and outdated; never use 14 as the toll. DHS breakdown: 6 drowning, 3 heart failure, 1 accidental gunshot, 1 self-inflicted gunshot (DOCUMENTED). **~$400 million** property damage in 1976 dollars (FACT — convergent); "$1B"/"$2B" figures are DISPUTED — never use. 1,014 homes destroyed, 2,620 severely damaged (DOCUMENTED). The dam was never rebuilt (FACT).

**Legacy.** Reclamation's Dam Safety program created (FACT); Safety of Dams Act of 1978, PL 95-578 (FACT); SEED evaluations (DOCUMENTED); the panel's technical maxim — design must **"render the inevitable leakage harmless"** (FACT — the signature closing line).

**The Fontenelle parallel.** In September 1965, the Bureau's Fontenelle Dam suffered a nearly identical right-abutment internal-erosion incident during first filling — saved only by emergency drawdown. The GAO found the similarities "striking" and said Teton's designers "did not benefit from the lessons learned from Fontenelle Dam" (DOCUMENTED). Reclamation's full account was suppressed until rediscovered in 2010. **This is the re-hook.**

## 2. THE CHAIN

### CONFIDENT SYSTEM
The Bureau of Reclamation — the agency that tamed the American West. 300+ major dams. Per the Department of the Interior, **not one fatal dam failure in its history — before or since Teton.** Teton Dam: authorized 1964, completed 1975, 305 feet of compacted earth, the Bureau's newest dam, filling for the very first time. Everything the Bureau knew about building dams said this one would stand.

### DECISION 1 — The site: build on fractured volcanic rock (1932–1964)
- **What happened:** After investigations from 1932, eight alternate sites examined, and ~100 borings at the chosen site, the Bureau selected the Teton River canyon; Congress authorized construction in 1964.
- **Who decided:** Bureau planners and designers; Congress (authorization).
- **What they knew:** The foundation was basalt and rhyolite — "not generally considered acceptable for structural foundations." The upper abutment rock was weathered, badly cracked volcanic rhyolite. Early investigations concluded seepage losses would be significant. Drilling fluid pumped into the rock in 1961–62 never returned — voids large enough to swallow construction material.
- **Why it seemed reasonable:** The region needed storage desperately — severe drought in 1961, severe flooding in 1962. The Bureau believed special engineering measures (grouting, key trenches) could master the geology. The Independent Panel itself later called the site studies "appropriate and extensive."
- **Evidence:** Independent Panel report; PL 88-583; panel on the borings.
- **Consequence:** Committed a 305-ft dam to one of the most difficult foundation geologies the Bureau had ever built on.

### DECISION 2 — The defense: trust the grout curtain (design, ~1969–1972)
- **What happened:** Seepage control rested on 70-ft-deep key trenches filled with compacted soil plus a single continuous grout curtain below them. No filter or drain zones at the critical dam–abutment contact.
- **Who decided:** USBR Office of Design and Construction, Denver.
- **What they knew:** The 1969 pilot grouting program needed more than double the estimated grout; some holes never reached refusal; follow-up sampling showed the rock as fractured as before. The panel confirms the pilot "forecast the difficulties to be experienced in construction of the final grout curtain."
- **Why it seemed reasonable:** Grouting was standard Bureau practice, refined over decades. The panel found "great effort was devoted to constructing a grout curtain of high quality" — "not inferior to many that have been considered acceptable on other projects." The pilot was read as proof the problem was solvable, not as a warning to stop.
- **Evidence:** Independent Panel conclusions 7–8.
- **Consequence:** "Complete dependence for seepage control on a combination of deep key trenches filled with windblown soils and a grout curtain" — with "inadequate provisions for collection and safe discharge of seepage or leakage which inevitably would occur." The panel's verdict: **"too much was expected of the grout curtain."**

### WARNING — The Cassandras (1972–1973) and the buried precedent (1965)
- USGS geologist David Schleicher (Dec 1972): warned of seismic/failure flooding that would make the 1962 flood "look like small potatoes" — and suggested placing motion-picture cameras to document it. His letter was downgraded to a preliminary report.
- Former Bureau geologist Shirley Pytlak (1973, federal court testimony): 300 gallons a minute vanishing into core-drilling holes — "if a drill hole leaks that much, what would an entire reservoir do?" Suit dismissed.
- Sierra Club attorney Tony Ruckel (~1973): the reservoir would leak more than reported; the Bureau withheld difficulty information. Environmentalists lost.
- **Fontenelle Dam, September 1965:** the Bureau's own dam suffered a nearly identical right-abutment internal-erosion failure during first filling — saved only by emergency drawdown. The full account was suppressed; the GAO later called the similarities to Teton "striking."
- **Why the warnings failed:** each came from outside the decision room (a geologist, a former employee, environmentalists) or was buried inside it (Fontenelle). None reached the designers as an instruction to stop.

### DECISION 3 — The core: erodible silt against fractured rock (design/construction)
- **What happened:** The impervious core and key-trench fill used wind-deposited clayey silts — low plasticity, highly erodible — placed against the heavily jointed abutment rock.
- **Who decided:** USBR designers (material selection); placed per specification by the contractor.
- **What they knew:** It was the locally available impervious material; the panel noted "the scarcity of more suitable materials."
- **Why it seemed reasonable:** The design "followed USBR practices, developed over many years from experience with other Bureau projects." Special compaction was specified as protection. It met the specifications as written. The construction team was formally exonerated — construction "conformed to the actual design in all significant aspects."
- **Evidence:** Independent Panel conclusions 4–5; Interior Review Group (secondary summary).
- **Consequence:** "The wind-deposited nonplastic to slightly plastic clayey silts… are highly erodible. The Panel considers that the use of this material adjacent to the heavily jointed rock of the abutment was a major factor contributing to the failure."

### DECISION 4 — The fill rate: 1 → 2 ft/day (March 23, 1976)
- **What happened:** With 150% of normal snowpack arriving and only the 850-cfs emergency outlet works operational, the project office received permission to double the filling rate.
- **Who decided:** The Teton project office with Bureau authorization.
- **What they knew:** The water was coming regardless; faster filling was the only way to manage it.
- **Why it seemed reasonable:** Operational necessity. There was no alternative reservoir to put the runoff in.
- **Evidence:** USBR project history (primary).
- **Consequence:** The reservoir reached critical elevation sooner — **but the panel found the rate was NOT a material cause**: "a similar failure would have occurred at some later date." **Script rule: present this as the reasonable decision critics wrongly blame — a correction beat, not an accusation.**

### DECISION 5 — June 5 morning: fight the leak, hold the alarm (7:00–10:45 a.m.)
- **What happened:** Crews attacked the leaks with dozers and riprap — the textbook response. At 9:30 a.m., project engineer Robison considered alerting downstream residents and decided the emergency was not imminent; he feared undue panic. Sheriffs were notified 10:00–10:45 a.m.; full evacuation warning ~11:00–11:30 a.m.
- **Who decided:** Robert R. Robison, Project Construction Engineer.
- **What they knew:** Leaks growing from 2 cfs to 50 cfs in hours; no precedent in Bureau experience for piping going from first muddy seep to full breach in four hours.
- **Why it seemed reasonable:** Every action was the correct procedure. Ordering 15,000+ people to flee on uncertain warning carries real costs — panic, and the warning might prove false. No Bureau dam had ever failed.
- **Evidence:** Robison's June 8, 1976 report; US DHS 2011; panel exoneration.
- **Consequence:** Warnings went out ~1 hour before the breach — and the evacuation, once ordered, worked remarkably: 10 of the 11 dead had been warned. **Script rule: Robison is never the villain. His 9:30 decision is the episode's most human beat — reasonable judgment under unknowable time pressure.**

### POINT OF NO RETURN — ~10:00–10:30 a.m.
The 15-cfs tunnel emerges **inside the embankment** at Sta. 15+25 — erosion is now in the dam body, not the foundation. Per the panel's physics, once piping "progressed rapidly through the main body of the dam," no field intervention available that morning could have arrested it. The 11:00 whirlpool and the 11:30 sinkhole are the surface expressions of a dam already lost.

### CATASTROPHE — 11:00–11:57 a.m.
11:00: whirlpool in the reservoir. 11:30: two dozers swallowed; operators pulled out by ropes. 11:55: crest collapse. **11:57: full breach.** A wall of water — contemporary press said 15 feet — moved down the canyon: Wilford and Sugar City ~1:00 p.m., Rexburg ~1:40 p.m. with ~80% of the city under water. 150–200 square miles flooded.

### INVESTIGATION
The Independent Panel (10 unaffiliated engineers, appointed 2 days after the failure; report December 1976): internal erosion (piping) deep in the right key trench — **but which initiation mechanism came first was never resolved, and the panel says it probably never will be.** Construction exonerated. Filling rate exonerated. The verdict: "a combination of geological factors and design decisions that, taken together, permitted the failure to develop" — and the maxim: design must **"render the inevitable leakage harmless."**

### LEGACY
Reclamation's Dam Safety program — now, per the Bureau, a world model. The Safety of Dams Act of 1978 (PL 95-578). Systematic SEED evaluations. Defensive design against internal erosion became standard practice worldwide: filters and drains, never complete reliance on a grout curtain. Teton remains the Bureau's **only** fatal dam failure. The site is visited by engineers from around the world. The dam was never rebuilt.

## 7. RE-HOOK (mandatory, 65–75% → placed ~11:30–12:00)

**Design:** new evidence + reversed interpretation, no artificial "but that's not all" language.

As the breach sequence ends (~11:30), the narration pivots on a documented fact:

> "The panel found the physical cause within months. But buried in the investigation was a second finding — about a different dam, eleven years earlier, on another Bureau river. In September 1965, Fontenelle Dam began to fail in almost exactly the same way: a right-abutment leak, internal erosion, during its first filling. Fontenelle was saved — by luck and by a fast drawdown. The Bureau's full account of what happened there was never published. A federal review later called the similarities 'striking' — and concluded Teton's designers never benefited from the lessons of Fontenelle."

**Why it works:** it reframes the story at the exact moment the viewer thinks it's over (breach shown, cause seemingly known). The interpretation reverses: this wasn't only geology plus design — the organization had already survived this failure once and failed to learn it. It introduces the parallel-case interlude (Fontenelle, ~60–90 seconds: the 1965 leak, the drawdown, the suppressed report, the GAO finding), then returns to the panel's Teton findings with new weight — and into the honesty beat (the unresolved mechanism). All labels DOCUMENTED CLAIM (secondary reporting of GAO) — attributed on screen/narration as "a later federal review found."

## 8. VOICEOVER — NARRATION BLUEPRINT

**Spec (measured bar, mandatory):**
- Third-person observer, always. Witness-guide, never protagonist. Zero first-person.
- **145–155 WPM**; utterance units of ~10–14 words (one breath, one idea). At ~16 minutes this yields roughly 2,320–2,480 words of narration.
- Restrained adjective discipline: dramatic adjectives ≤ ~1.5 per 1,000 words. Dread from facts. Zero exclamation culture — no exclamation marks in the script, ever.
- **No rhetorical questions in the voiceover.** Not one. Questions live in the title, thumbnail, and chapters.
- Factual ballast spoken naturally: dates, times, measurements, tolls, report names ("the Independent Panel," "the Bureau's project history") as credibility markers — never as recitation.
- Register: plain-language story-led by default. Technical-dense only inside the mechanism passages (piping, grout curtain, key trench, hydraulic fracturing) — one register per passage, never mixed within a paragraph.
- Contractions at a natural conversational level; never slangy, never stiff.
- Cold open = date + paradox, three flat sentences (the selected hook, verbatim).
- Proper nouns pronounced correctly — mispronounced names are an instant credibility failure in this genre. (Verification list for VO session: Teton, Rexburg, Wilford, Sugar City, Fremont, Robison, Schleicher, Pytlak, Ruckel, Andrus, Fontenelle, Sta. = "station.")

**Style samples (illustrative of the register — not script lines):**
- Doing it right: "The water coming out of the dam was clear. For an earthen dam on its first filling, that was not unexpected. It was also the last normal thing anyone saw that week."
- Doing it wrong: "What happened next would shock the entire nation!" / "But have you ever wondered how a dam could just… disappear?" / "This was an UNBELIEVABLE catastrophe!!!"

**The documentary-ear test (hard gate):** if a listener can identify the voice as synthetic — robotic cadence, choppy phrasing, mispronounced proper nouns, breathless short chunks — the video does not ship, regardless of script quality.

## 10. EDITING PLAN

**Governing rhythm: FAST CHAIN — SLOW CATASTROPHE — SOBER LEGACY.**

- **Shot changes:** brisk through the chain sections (new visual idea per utterance unit, ~12-word cadence → roughly a visual change every 5–6 seconds in 3:00–10:00); deliberately slowed in 10:00–13:00 (holds of 8–12 seconds on the breach photography).
- **Ken Burns movement:** every still gets directional movement — push-in on the breach (menace approaching), pull-back on construction photos (scale), lateral drift on maps (journey). No static frame held past its welcome outside the catastrophe section.
- **Diagram reveals:** the cross-section builds progressively — rock first, then trench, then grout curtain, then the Sta. 14+00 marker — each element appearing as the narration reaches it. The mechanism cutaway animates the erosion tunnel growing in sync with the June 5 clock.
- **Timeline progression:** the chain graphic from 0:20 fills link by link; the June 5 clock (7:00 → 8:30 → 9:30 → 10:00 → 11:00 → 11:30 → 11:55 → 11:57) is the section's spine.
- **Text beats (on screen, thesis-style):** "TOO MUCH WAS EXPECTED OF THE GROUT CURTAIN" (panel's words) · "POINT OF NO RETURN — ~10:00 A.M." · "11:57 A.M." · "RENDER THE INEVITABLE LEAKAGE HARMLESS" · "THE ELEVEN" (remembrance). Five beats maximum — each must earn its screen time.
- **Sound-design opportunities:** low drone under 6:00–10:00 escalation, rising almost imperceptibly; water/erosion foley under the mechanism animation (subtle, never cartoonish); **near-silence at 11:57** — narration pauses a full beat over the breach photograph (the observed "empty radar screen" device: absence as emphasis).
- **Silence moments:** two scripted — (1) the 11:57 breach beat (~4 seconds of near-silence); (2) before the remembrance name cards (~3 seconds). Silence is mixed deliberately, not as dead air.
- **Mechanism animation:** the single most important produced asset — internal erosion visualized: water entering the joint beneath the grout cap, the tunnel growing through erodible fill, the 15-cfs tunnel emerging in the embankment. Must be technically faithful to the panel's description (key trench, Sta. 14+00–15+25) — reviewed against the claim ledger before render.
- **Catastrophe pacing:** decelerated. Longer holds, fewer cuts, wider shots. The fireball-montage instinct is banned — spectacle is decelerated, the chain is accelerated.
- **Legacy pacing:** sober and steady — document close-ups, present-day imagery, text beats. Resolved tone, never triumphant.

**Do not imitate** any channel's distinctive editing style (no Brick Immortar moral-title cards, no Salvage Room real-time gimmickry here, no Dark Records spectacle cutting). The editing identity is: *the diagram builds as the chain builds.*
